Motor Setup & CAN IDs

Picks up
The mechanism is powered, the firmware is current and Tuner X sees every device; nothing has turned yet.
Hands on
Voltage Out pushed a volt with no target and no place to stop; PID Tuning gives the motor a target.
Your turn
None.
On screen
The lesson page, filmed: lessons/mechanism-setup/page.json.
  1. intro

    Your bench is powered and showing up in Tuner X, and nothing has turned yet. Before anything does, each device needs its own number and the right settings.

  2. ids

    These are the numbers the rest of this page uses. Set the rows for the hardware you've got.

  3. dup

    A factory default wipes nearly everything, but it leaves the ID alone. So if two devices flash on one blink, they're sharing a number, and one has to move first.

  4. blink

    Blink is how you know which device you're about to change. A name in a list proves nothing until a light on the bench flashes back.

  5. apply

    None of it is saved until you press Apply. It's a small arrow, and it writes settings to the device, not firmware.

  6. motor

    On the arm, direction comes first, and you check it by hand. The motor's position should climb as you turn the arm counterclockwise, with the motor facing you.

  7. encoder

    The encoder gets the same hand test. Then you pick the spot that counts as zero, and a quarter turn from there should read about a quarter of a rotation.

  8. rotations

    Rotations, not degrees. Counterclockwise is positive, and every position target from here on is written that way.

  9. link

    Only now do you link the two. Link them while they count in opposite directions and every lesson after this one breaks, so finish with one more turn by hand.

  10. voltage

    Now the first motion under power. Voltage Out sends a fixed voltage with no target, and it won't stop at the end of travel. You stop it.

  11. safe

    So start at one volt and enable for about a second. Blink first, too, because the voltage goes to an ID, not to the device you had in mind.

  12. wrong

    If the arm turns clockwise, don't fix it here. It means a hand test was skipped or never applied, so go back and redo them.

  13. close

    Power the bench down, bring it back, and walk these checks. Once positive means the same direction by hand and under power, the motor is ready for a target. That's PID tuning.

PID Tuning in Tuner X

Picks up
Voltage Out pushed one volt with no target and no place to stop; now the motor gets a target.
Hands on
The target here jumps all at once; Motion Magic walks it there instead, on the same gains.
Your turn
A scripted demo of the page's interactive.
On screen
The lesson page, filmed: lessons/pid-control/page.json.
  1. intro

    Last time you sent the motor one volt, and it had no idea where to stop. Now it gets a target, and the motor itself does the work of getting there.

  2. guide

    CTRE's own tuning guide is the one to follow. The steps here are the same method rewritten in volts, for when you can't reach it.

  3. play

    Before you touch the real mechanism, break this one. Every gain starts at zero, and a bad number costs you nothing.

  4. tryit

    Try it. Push the proportional gain up until the arm swings past its target, and watch the trace.narration pauses: your turn

  5. units

    Every gain on this page is volts for each rotation. A number copied from a guide that uses different units won't mean the same thing here.

  6. plot

    The plot is how you'll judge every change, so set it up first. Both signals go in one group, and the timespan comes way down, because it's counted in minutes.

  7. apply

    A gain you type does nothing until you press Apply. If a change seems to have no effect, check that before you change it again.

  8. zero

    Start with every gain at zero. Whatever the mechanism does after that, you'll know which number caused it.

  9. forward

    Feedforward comes first. Find the smallest and the largest gravity gain that hold the arm level, and what you want sits in the middle.

  10. feedback

    Then feedback. Keep doubling the proportional gain until it overshoots, back off to about half, and let a little derivative take out the rest.

  11. shapes

    When it goes wrong, read the plot. Error that keeps growing means a direction is backwards, so disable right away. A hum at rest is too much gain, and more damping won't fix it.

  12. close

    You're done when three runs each way look alike. The target still jumps there all at once, though, and smoothing that out is the next lesson.

Your turn

On screen
The lesson page, filmed: lessons/motion-magic/page.json.
  1. intro

    Your gains are saved, and three runs each way look alike. The target still jumps to the end in one step, though, so the loop gets asked for the whole move at once.

  2. same

    None of that tuning gets thrown away. Motion Magic only changes the target your gains are chasing, so keep the same plot open and leave Slot 0 alone.

  3. limits

    Two limits shape the trip. One is a top speed, and the other is how quickly the mechanism may build up to it. Jerk you can ignore unless your mechanism is very heavy.

  4. kinds

    An arm is going somewhere, so its plan includes the stop. A flywheel never arrives anywhere, which is why it only gets a ramp.

  5. small

    Start much slower than you think you need, because the first run is only there to show you what the profile does. Check the units too. They count turns of the mechanism, not the motor.

  6. mode

    Nothing changes until you switch the control mode over on the Control tab. The limits themselves go in the Motion Magic section of the configs.

  7. enable

    Switching the mode also disabled the motor. If you press Apply and nothing moves, that's the reason, so enable it again before you run anything.

  8. raise

    Once it follows the profile cleanly on every run, raise the limits a step at a time. You stop when the measurement falls behind, the current spikes, or the structure shakes.

  9. moves

    Then try the longest move you'll ever ask for, both ways, and the shortest. A short one never gets up to cruising speed, so what it really tests is the stop.

  10. margin

    Don't keep the best numbers you found. A battery late in a match can't keep up with what it managed fresh, so back the limits off and leave some room.

  11. close

    Five runs with the traces stacked on each other, and the motor plans its own way there. All of it happened by clicking. Next you start on Java, so code can ask for the same move.

The Command Framework

Picks up
Java Basics ended on a method that builds a command and hands it back with the lambda parked inside, for something else to run later.
Hands on
Project Setup generates the robot project that the scheduler call, the mechanisms and the mode classes all go into.
Your turn
None.
On screen
The lesson page, filmed: lessons/command-framework/page.json.
  1. intro

    Last time, a command came back with its code parked inside, waiting for something else to run it. Here's that something else.

  2. parts

    Each part answers a question. A trigger is when and a mechanism is what moves, with a command in between saying how. The coroutine is just the steps inside that command.

  3. tick

    None of them runs by itself. Fifty times a second the robot calls this one method, and the single line inside it gives the scheduler its turn.

  4. job

    Each turn it checks every trigger, then starts and cancels commands from what it found. Delete that line and nothing on the robot ever runs.

  5. owns

    A command built inside a mechanism owns it while it runs, which keeps two commands from driving one motor at once.

  6. priority

    So what happens when a second command wants the arm? Every command here has the same priority, so the newcomer takes it and the old one is canceled.

  7. cancel

    Canceled doesn't mean stopped. When nothing owns the arm, nothing is talking to its motor, and it carries on with the last request it was given.

  8. robot

    Mechanisms and the driver's controller get created once, in the same file as the scheduler call, and they're public so every mode can reach them.

  9. modes

    What the robot does with them is up to a mode. Each is its own class, and the marking on top puts it in the driver station's list.

  10. bind

    Button bindings go in the mode's constructor. The class is built when someone picks the mode and thrown away on a switch, so a binding from auto can't fire in teleop.

  11. forever

    Most commands in this course run their body every loop and never finish, so something outside has to end them.

  12. release

    Here a trigger does it. Letting go cancels the running command, and the second half of the line starts the one that stops the motor.

  13. close

    Before you move on, work out who owns the arm before the press, during the hold and after the release. Next you'll make the project all this code goes into.

    Get one backwards and the robot heads the wrong way at full speed, so the first check on this page happens before anything drives.

  5. map

    Here's the idea the lesson rests on. The camera is slow, and it loses the tag up close, so a sighting only has to put the tag down. The wheels carry the robot from there.

  6. gates

    The new call runs ahead of the trust checks from last time. One close tag is exactly what those throw out, and it's the one sighting this needs.

  7. remember

    Each sighting looks up where the robot was when the picture was taken, then places the tag from there. It also logs a number you'll read later.

  8. drive

    The driving is the builder you already know from Drive to Point. What changed is where it gets its pose, which now comes from the tag.

  9. empty

    Notice the check before it drives. If no camera has seen that tag yet, the command writes a message and quits, and the robot stays put.

  10. record

    Don't reach for a tape measure. Park the robot by hand where it should score, read the numbers the camera gives you, and type those in. Whatever's off about the camera's mount cancels out.

  11. event

    Then do it again at the event, before your first match. Tags are mounted by hand, so every field differs a little.

  12. close

    The third check is the one only this lesson has. Cover the camera, shove the robot sideways so its field pose is wrong, and it still stops on the same spot. That's the main course done, and mechanism simulation is an extra.

On screen
The lesson page, filmed: lessons/mechanism-simulation/page.json.
  1. intro

    Everything you've written so far ran on a real motor on the bench. If your own robot isn't built yet, the same code can run against a physics model instead.

  2. two

    You've used Hardware Sim, where the laptop stood in for the robot controller. Here the motors are simulated too, and a simulated motor has nothing to turn.

  3. numbers

    The file to read is the sim class. It opens with the real arm written down as numbers, and the simulation is only as good as they are.

  4. model

    The physics comes from the library, an arm swinging on one joint under gravity. It asks for every number up front, which means there's no setting you can forget.

  5. loop

    Every loop is three steps. The motor's voltage goes into the model, the model moves the arm forward one tick, and the result goes back into the simulated sensors for your code to read.

  6. hook

    Two calls at the end of the constructor hook it in. The picture runs everywhere, because it only draws what the motor reports. The sim gets built only when there's no real arm.

  7. owner

    Nothing gets added to the robot class. The sim only lasts as long as whatever built it, so a mechanism made inside an OpMode stops simulating when that OpMode ends.

  8. gains

    Run it as it ships and nothing moves, because every gain starts at zero. Put in your own starting values first.

  9. see

    Once you're enabled, open the arm's picture. Hold a button, and a thin grey line shows where the closed loop is trying to take it.

  10. tune

    Tune in the same order as in Tuner X, changing the number in code and restarting between tries. The result fits a model, not your mechanism.

  11. motor

    For your own design, the motor changes in two places. The simulated Talon assumes a different one unless told, and left that way, the bench arm ran away from its target.

  12. close

    You're done when the orange line settles on the grey one. That's the end of the course, and the next arm you tune should be a real one.
