Three Parts and a Bus

Format
recording + 10 s animated opener
Picks up
The start of the course: nothing installed but Tuner X.
Hands on
'Red means two devices share an ID' hands to Motor Setup; the name canivore is what the code matches in Mechanisms.
Your turn
None. A procedure, recorded.
On screen
Animated: laptop → USB → CANivore → devices, a greyed 'robot controller' the packet skips. Recorded: CANivore USB, localhost, rename (staged), batch firmware, power cycle. Card colours as a still strip (only green can be shown live).
  1. intro

    Every mechanism in this workshop is three parts: a motor, a sensor, and a bus that connects them to your laptop.

  2. motor

    The motor has its controller built into the back. It runs its own control loop and reports its own position, so the motor is a sensor too.

  3. sensor

    The arm also gets an absolute encoder. It still knows the arm's angle after the power's been off all week. The flywheel doesn't need one.

  4. bus

    The CANivore gives those devices their own bus, and your laptop plugs straight into it. There's no robot in between, so you can start right now.

  5. connect

    Power the mechanism first, then plug in the USB. In Tuner X, turn on CANivore USB, and set the address to localhost.over recorded tool footage

  6. name

    Your CANivore shows up. Rename it canivore, all lowercase. Every line of code in this course uses that name, so spell it exactly the same.over recorded tool footage

  7. firmware

    Old firmware still connects, and then it fails later in ways that come and go. Put every device on the same version.over recorded tool footage

  8. colors

    The card's color tells you where you stand. Green is current. Yellow means there's an update. Red means two devices share an ID, and that's the next lesson.

  9. check

    Power cycle everything. The name and the firmware live on the devices, so they come back exactly as you left them.over recorded tool footage

  10. close

    Three parts, one bus, and a name that matches the code.

One Motion, Then Silence

Format
hybrid, about 40% animated
Picks up
Voltage Out had no target; now the motor gets one.
Hands on
The three shapes (runs away, buzzes, falls short) are called back by Drive to Point and Profiled Drive to Point. Hands off to the page's MechanismPlayground rather than duplicating it.
Your turn
Pick a kP that's too low, then too high, and read the plot. Mirrors the playground; the video's version is three fixed choices.
On screen
Animated: gravity arrow shrinking with cos(angle) and the kG counter-arrow matching it; the three kP shapes as arm + trace, then the arm stripped away; a stacked output bar (feedforward slab, feedback sliver). Recorded: plot setup, Slot 0 to zero, enable on zero gains, kG/kP/kD steps with every value masked, apply and power cycle.
  1. intro

    The motor runs its own control loop. Tuner X just sends it a target, and draws what happens.

  2. zero

    Start with every gain at zero, and enable. Nothing moves. That's correct: zero gains mean zero volts.over recorded tool footage

  3. gravity

    Gravity pulls hardest when the arm is level, and not at all when it's straight up.

  4. kg

    The gravity gain cancels exactly that pull, at every angle. Raise it until the arm holds level on its own.over recorded tool footage

  5. ks

    The static gain covers friction. Set it to half the gap between the smallest and largest gravity gains that hold.

  6. kp

    Now the feedback. Double the proportional gain until the arm overshoots, then cut it in half.over recorded tool footage

  7. kd

    The derivative gain takes out the overshoot. Too much of it, and the arm buzzes.

  8. tryit

    Try it. Pick a gain that's too low, then one that's too high, and watch the plot.narration pauses: your turn

  9. shapes

    Three shapes tell you what's wrong. It runs away, it buzzes, or it falls short. Read the plot, not the mechanism.

  10. apply

    Apply the gains, and power cycle. They're saved on the motor.over recorded tool footage

  11. close

    Feedforward pays for holding. Feedback fixes what's left. A tuned arm makes one motion, and then it's silent.

Names the 'someone' who runs the command: the scheduler.
Your turn
Two keys: C calls the method (a sealed package lands in a command, arm stays still), S runs the command (package opens every loop, arm moves).

The Scheduler

Picks up
Last video's package needed something to run it.
Hands on
Lanes and owners are reused by every later video; the empty lane before the first press is left unexplained on purpose (latched owns it).
Your turn
Hold either trigger or both; each mechanism's lane changes owner independently.
On screen
When/What/How cards; a 50-per-second tick clock; one lane per mechanism (Arm, Flywheel) with command blocks; controller with both triggers; Robot.robotPeriodic and the MyTeleop bindings; flywheel drawable (new).
  1. intro

    Robot code comes down to three things. Triggers say when. Mechanisms are what moves. Commands say how.

  2. callback

    Last time, a command was a package waiting for something to run it. That something is the scheduler.

  3. tick

    Fifty times a second, the robot calls one line, and the scheduler takes a turn. It checks every trigger, starts and cancels commands, and runs whatever's scheduled.

  4. lanes

    Each mechanism gets a lane, and a lane holds one command at a time.

  5. press

    Hold the left trigger. The command that pushes at six volts takes the arm's lane. It owns the arm now, and nothing else can drive that motor.

  6. hold

    That command never decides to stop on its own. Ending it is the trigger's job.

  7. release

    Let go, and in a single tick it's canceled, and the stop command takes the lane.

  8. tryit

    Your turn. Hold one trigger, then both. Each mechanism has its own lane, so each one has its own owner.narration pauses: your turn

  9. newcomer

    If a second command asks for a lane that's already taken, the newcomer wins, and the old one is canceled.

  10. close

    Fifty times a second, the scheduler decides which one command owns each mechanism.

12 · Workshop 4 · Routines · /chaining-commands · ~86 s

Steps

Picks up
Every command so far is a hold that runs until you let go.
Hands on
A one-second timeout is a guess; Finish Conditions replaces it with the sensor.
Your turn
Remove the timeout chip from the first command and press Y again. The flywheel never starts.
On screen
Introduces the scheduler timeline used for all of Workshop 4: lanes per mechanism, bars that end vs bars with no right edge, a playhead, a bracket for the group, a stopwatch chip for withTimeout. Arm stays up after its step (latched position).
  1. intro

    A routine is a list of commands that run in order.

  2. callback

    Every command you've written so far is a hold. Hold the button, and it runs until you let go. On this timeline, that's a bar with no end.

  3. problem

    Put a hold first in a list, and the list never moves on. There's no error. It just looks frozen.

  4. step

    So give it an ending. A one second timeout turns the hold into a step.

  5. sequence

    Now the list works. The arm goes up, and one second later the flywheel starts.

  6. stays

    When the arm's step ends, the arm stays up. Its position request is still on the motor.

  7. last

    The flywheel is still a hold, so the whole routine is a hold too. Letting go of the button is what ends it.

  8. tryit

    Try it. Take the timeout off, and run it again.narration pauses: your turn

  9. never

    The flywheel never starts. The list is still waiting on a command that never ends.

  10. race

    A race is the other way to combine commands. They run side by side, and the race ends when the first one finishes. So a hold can never win one.

  11. close

    Give every step an ending. The last one decides whether the whole list ends.

13 · Workshop 4 · Routines · /finish-conditions · ~81 s

Ask Every Loop

Picks up
Last video's one-second timeout was a guess.
Hands on
'A timeout is not arrival' is what Coroutines checks for with timedOut().
Your turn
Drag the tolerance down to 0.001 degrees and run it. The stopwatch wins at two seconds; the next step starts with the arm short.
On screen
Arm angle gauge with a target tick and a shaded tolerance band; the condition line pulsing each loop with a true/false chip; on the timeline, the step drawn as a race between a condition bar and a stopwatch bar.
  1. intro

    A finish condition ends a step when the mechanism says it got there.

  2. callback

    Last time, the arm's step ended after one second. That number was a guess.

  3. question

    This method asks the sensor whether the arm is close enough to its target. Close enough, because a real arm is never exactly on the number.

  4. every

    The arrow matters again. It hands the question over, so it gets asked every loop, and the step ends on the loop the answer turns true.

  5. timeout

    Back it with a timeout. Now the step has two ways to end: the arm arrives, or the clock runs out.

  6. race

    On most runs, the sensor wins in about half a second.

  7. tryit

    Try it. Squeeze the tolerance down to almost nothing, and run it again.narration pauses: your turn

  8. lose

    The arm gets close, but never close enough, so the clock wins at two seconds.

  9. proves

    And the next step starts anyway, with the arm short of its target. A timeout tells you the waiting is over. It doesn't tell you where the arm is.

  10. close

    End on the sensor, back it with a timer, and never treat the timer running out as arriving.

14 · Workshop 4 · Routines · /coroutines · ~84 s

Fork and Wait

Picks up
Autonomous: nobody is holding a button, so nobody can let go.
Hands on
The routine gives up silently after three seconds, which is Logging's cold open.
Your turn
Line one is a two-way chip: fork or await. Await freezes on line one while the 15 second auto clock drains; fork carries on.
On screen
Matches the existing CoroutineTimeline component on /coroutines: main flow column, peel-off lanes for Arm and Flywheel, solid = running, dashed = waiting, two exits from a wait (arrived / gave up), a 15 s autonomous clock, driver station auto picker showing 'Raise And Shoot'. Controller idle on purpose.
  1. intro

    A coroutine is a routine written as plain code, one line after another, that can pause on a line.

  2. callback

    In autonomous, nobody's holding a button. There's nobody to let go.

  3. why

    And the arm has to keep holding while everything after it happens. A list can't do that.

  4. fork

    Fork starts a command and moves straight on. The arm's lane keeps running on its own.

  5. wait

    Then the routine waits until the arm is in place, but never longer than three seconds.

  6. tryit

    Your turn. Fork the arm, or await it. Pick one, and run it.narration pauses: your turn

  7. await

    Await waits for the command to finish. The arm holds forever, so the routine never gets past the first line.

  8. timeout

    If that wait runs out, the routine checks, and leaves. Without the check, it would shoot at an angle nobody looked at.

  9. shoot

    Then the flywheel spins up, the routine waits for it to reach speed, and the shot gets one second.

  10. stop

    Last, it forks a stop for the flywheel. Ending a routine cancels everything it started, and canceling doesn't stop a motor.
